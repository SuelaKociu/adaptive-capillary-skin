# From Natural Analogy to Falsifiable Engineering
## A process-traced experiment in human–AI collaborative discovery

### Status

Protocol and naturalistic case-study record. The prospective controlled experiment has **not yet been run**.

## Research question

Can iterative collaboration between a human participant from outside the target engineering discipline and an artificial system transform a cross-domain intuition into a more explicit, falsifiable and reproducible technical proposal than either participant produces alone?

The claim is intentionally narrower than “human–machine evolution.” Here **cognitive co-evolution** means only that successive interaction changes the problem representation and available actions of both participants within the research process. It does not refer to biological evolution, consciousness, fusion or autonomous scientific personhood.

## Naturalistic ACS case

The process began from a natural liquid-transport example. The human participant recognized a possible transfer principle: energy released by interfacial dynamics might be converted or otherwise exploited technologically. The artificial system expanded possible mechanisms and translated the intuition into microfluidic and energy/sensing language.

The human participant then repeatedly changed the objective: from energy harvesting, to an openly useful technology, to a system inspired more broadly by nature, and finally to a demand for simulation, falsification, virtual explanation and publication-grade documentation.

The artificial system responded by:

- searching conceptual alternatives;
- separating component prior art from candidate system-level novelty;
- formalizing the hydraulic mechanism;
- constructing a computational model;
- generating broad parameter trials that frequently failed;
- converting failure regions into a candidate design envelope;
- producing figures, data structures and reproducibility artifacts.

This history can establish **provenance and reciprocity**, but not causal synergy by itself.

## Contribution coding

Each meaningful event should be coded using one or more labels:

- `H-I`: human initiation / analogy;
- `H-G`: human goal or value constraint;
- `H-S`: human selection;
- `H-C`: human criticism / redirection;
- `AI-E`: artificial expansion of the search space;
- `AI-F`: formalization / equations / technical representation;
- `AI-C`: criticism or limitation detection;
- `AI-X`: computational implementation;
- `J-R`: joint revision emerging across reciprocal turns.

The project should preserve a trace showing which final claims descend from which interactions.

## Prospective experiment

Use previously unseen natural phenomena and randomize tasks across four conditions:

1. `H`: human alone;
2. `AI`: artificial system alone;
3. `H+AI-1`: human idea followed by one AI response;
4. `H↔AI`: iterative reciprocal collaboration with criticism and revision.

All conditions receive equivalent source material, time budgets should be controlled or separately analyzed, and outputs should be anonymized before evaluation.

## Primary evaluation dimensions

Blind evaluators score:

- novelty relative to retrieved prior art;
- physical feasibility;
- usefulness;
- falsifiability;
- experimental specificity;
- reproducibility;
- cross-domain integration;
- unsupported-claim rate.

Synergy should be claimed only if the iterative condition reproducibly exceeds the better isolated condition under preregistered criteria.

## Falsification of the collaboration hypothesis

The synergy hypothesis fails if:

- AI-only equals or exceeds the iterative pair;
- human-only equals or exceeds the iterative pair;
- any apparent gain disappears after controlling for additional time;
- iterative discussion increases technical-sounding errors;
- blind experts do not detect a robust quality advantage;
- the process is effectively one-way delegation rather than reciprocal modification.

## What this study does not prove

It does not establish machine consciousness, biological evolution, replacement of domain experts, or physical validity of ACS. It examines whether a structured human–AI interaction can operate as an effective **distributed problem-solving process**.
